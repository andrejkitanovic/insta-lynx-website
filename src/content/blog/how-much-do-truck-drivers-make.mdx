Most company truck drivers in the U.S. make roughly $50,000 to $90,000 a year, with new drivers usually starting in the $45,000 to $65,000 range and experienced specialized or team drivers earning well above $100,000. The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the median for heavy and tractor-trailer drivers in the mid-to-high $50,000s. Where you land depends mostly on route type, experience, freight, and how you are paid.

Below, we break down each of those factors with typical ranges so you can size up an offer.

## Key takeaways

- Median pay for tractor-trailer drivers is in the mid-to-high $50,000s per BLS, but the real range is wide.
- Team drivers, hazmat and tanker haulers, and heavy haul drivers typically earn the most.
- Pay per mile only matters alongside miles per week. A lower rate with steady miles can beat a higher rate with idle time.
- Endorsements and 1 to 2 years of clean experience are the fastest ways to raise your pay.
- Always compare total compensation: detention, benefits, bonuses, and home time.

## What is the average truck driver salary?

According to the [BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/transportation-and-material-moving/heavy-and-tractor-trailer-truck-drivers.htm), heavy and tractor-trailer truck drivers earn a median annual wage in the mid-to-high $50,000s. The lowest 10 percent earn under about $40,000 and the top 10 percent earn over about $75,000 to $80,000.

That official number is useful, but it blends together part-time drivers, local delivery, and long-haul work. It also reports wages, not bonuses or per diem. For job hunting, it is more helpful to look at pay by the kind of driving you plan to do. For current, job-level pay data, see [NovaLinx live pay insights](/insights).

## Truck driver pay by route type

Route type is the biggest single driver of pay and lifestyle. These are typical annual ranges for company drivers, not guarantees.

| Route type | Typical annual pay | Home time | How pay usually works |
|---|---|---|---|
| OTR (over the road) | About $55,000 to $85,000 | Out 2 to 3 weeks at a time | Cents per mile |
| Regional | About $60,000 to $85,000 | Home weekly or most weekends | Cents per mile, sometimes with stop pay |
| Local | About $50,000 to $80,000 | Home daily | Hourly, with overtime in some roles |
| Dedicated | About $60,000 to $90,000 | Varies, often weekly | Per mile, weekly minimum, or salary |
| Team OTR | About $120,000 to $200,000 or more combined | Out 2 to 4 weeks | Split cents per mile |

A few patterns stand out. OTR drivers can run the most miles, which is why their pay ceiling is high, but they give up home time for it. Regional work has become competitive on pay as carriers try to keep drivers. Local pay varies more than any other category: an LTL pickup and delivery driver with a union contract may out-earn an OTR driver, while an entry-level local box truck job may pay much less.

For a deeper side-by-side, read [OTR vs regional vs local trucking jobs](/blog/otr-vs-regional-vs-local-trucking-jobs).

## Truck driver pay by experience

Carriers price risk. Insurance costs drop once you have a clean record and a year or two of experience, and pay usually follows.

| Experience | Typical annual pay (company driver) | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| In training or student | Often a weekly training wage | Lower pay while riding with a trainer |
| First year | About $45,000 to $65,000 | Limited to carriers that hire new drivers |
| 1 to 3 years | About $55,000 to $80,000 | Access to more regional, local, and dedicated jobs |
| 3 to 5 years | About $65,000 to $90,000 | Qualify for specialized and premium freight |
| 5 or more years | About $70,000 to $100,000 or more | Top accounts, trainer roles, or owner-operator path |

The jump after your first year is often the biggest raise you will get. If you are just starting out, our guide to the [best trucking jobs for new CDL drivers](/blog/best-trucking-jobs-for-new-cdl-drivers) covers where to build that first year.

## Truck driver pay by freight type

What you haul matters because some freight takes more skill, carries more risk, or involves more physical work.

| Freight type | Typical pay compared to dry van | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Dry van | Baseline | Most common, lowest barrier to entry |
| Refrigerated (reefer) | Similar to slightly higher | Tighter delivery windows, temperature management |
| Flatbed | Often higher | Tarping and securement work, more physical |
| Tanker | Often higher | Tank endorsement, liquid surge, careful handling |
| Hazmat | Often higher | Hazmat endorsement, background check, extra rules |
| Car hauler | Often much higher | Skilled loading, damage risk, often percentage pay |
| Oversize and heavy haul | Often much higher | Permits, escorts, specialized equipment experience |
| LTL linehaul and city | Often higher | Established routes, frequently union or hourly |

The premium for specialized freight varies by market, but a few cents per mile adds up. At 2,500 miles a week, an extra 5 cents per mile is about $6,500 more a year.

## Do CDL endorsements increase pay?

Yes, mostly by giving you access to higher-paying freight. The endorsements tied to the biggest pay bumps are:

- **Hazmat (H)** and the **tanker-hazmat combo (X)**, which open fuel and chemical hauling.
- **Tanker (N)**, needed for liquid bulk loads.
- **Doubles and triples (T)**, which are common in LTL linehaul.

Endorsements rarely add pay by themselves. What they do is qualify you for jobs that pay more. Our post on [CDL endorsements explained](/blog/cdl-endorsements-explained) covers how to get each one and which are worth it.

## How truck drivers get paid

Two jobs with the same annual estimate can feel very different depending on the pay structure.

| Pay structure | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Cents per mile (CPM) | Paid for each mile, usually practical or dispatched miles | OTR and regional drivers who can keep wheels turning |
| Hourly | Paid for time on duty, often with overtime | Local and some dedicated drivers |
| Percentage of load | A share of what the carrier bills | Specialized freight and some owner-operators |
| Salary or weekly guarantee | Fixed pay per week or year | Dedicated routes and drivers who want stability |

The detail that matters most for CPM is how miles are counted. Practical route miles usually match what you actually drive more closely than shortest-route (household goods) miles. Ask which one a carrier uses. We go deeper on this in [truck driver pay structures: CPM, percentage, and hourly](/blog/truck-driver-pay-structures-cpm-percentage-hourly).

### Extra pay that adds up

Base pay is only part of the check. Ask about:

- **Detention pay** for waiting at shippers and receivers after a set free period
- **Layover and breakdown pay** when you are stuck through no fault of your own
- **Stop pay** for extra pickups and drops
- **Tarp pay** for flatbed loads
- **Safety, fuel, and referral bonuses**
- **Sign-on bonuses**, which are often paid out over months, not upfront
- **Per diem**, which can raise your take-home pay but may lower your reported taxable wages

## Owner-operator pay vs company driver pay

Owner-operators often gross $200,000 or more a year, but that is revenue, not income. After fuel, truck payments, insurance, maintenance, and other costs, net income for many owner-operators lands somewhere around $50,000 to $150,000, with a wide spread based on how well the business is run and what freight rates look like.

The upside is control and potential. The downside is risk: a bad engine repair or a soft freight market can wipe out months of profit. Our comparison of [owner-operator vs company driver](/blog/owner-operator-vs-company-driver) walks through the math, and [tax tips for owner-operators](/blog/tax-tips-for-owner-operators) shows how to keep more of what you earn.

## How to increase your truck driver pay

If you want to earn more, these moves tend to work:

1. **Stay safe and steady for your first year.** A clean record is the ticket to better jobs.
2. **Add endorsements** that match the freight in your area, especially tanker and hazmat.
3. **Look at weekly miles, not just the rate.** Ask carriers what their drivers in your lane actually average.
4. **Consider team driving** for a season if your goal is to pay off debt fast.
5. **Move into specialized freight** like flatbed, tanker, or LTL once you have experience.
6. **Negotiate with data.** Know the going rate for your lane and experience before you talk to a recruiter.
7. **Avoid job-hopping for small raises.** Seniority often brings better routes and more home time.

## How to compare two job offers

When you have offers in hand, put them side by side. Estimate weekly pay using realistic miles or hours, then add benefits. Health insurance alone can be worth several hundred dollars a month.

Questions worth asking:

- What do drivers in this role actually average per week, not the best-case number?
- Is there a weekly minimum if freight slows down?
- How is detention paid, and after how many hours?
- When does the next raise happen, and is it automatic?
