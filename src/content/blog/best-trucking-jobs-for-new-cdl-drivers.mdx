The best trucking jobs for new CDL drivers are usually OTR or regional dry van and reefer positions, dedicated accounts that accept recent graduates, and company-sponsored training programs, because those carriers are set up to train and insure drivers with no experience. The right first job is the one that gives you safe, supported miles and a clean record for year two, not the highest advertised pay. This guide covers the jobs that hire new drivers, how training contracts work, what to look for, and how to get through your first year.

## Key takeaways

- Your first CDL job is about **building experience and a clean record**. That opens far better jobs after about a year.
- **OTR and regional dry van or reefer** jobs hire the most drivers with no experience.
- **Company-sponsored training** can make getting a CDL cheaper up front, but usually requires a work commitment. Know what you owe if you leave.
- Look for **paid training**, a clear trainer period, reasonable weekly miles, and written terms.
- Most new drivers struggle in the first 90 days. Planning for sleep, food, money, and communication with dispatch makes a real difference.

## Why many trucking companies want experience, and who does not

Insurance is the main reason. Carriers pay more to insure inexperienced drivers, and many local and specialized jobs involve tight city docks, heavy traffic, or difficult freight. That is why so many postings ask for one or two years of verifiable experience.

The good news is that many carriers specifically recruit new CDL holders. They run structured training programs, pair new drivers with trainers, and plan for the learning curve. Your goal is to find one of those carriers that also treats new drivers fairly.

## Best types of trucking jobs for new CDL drivers

| Job type | How often it hires new drivers | Typical home time | Pros for new drivers | Cons for new drivers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| OTR dry van | Very often | Out 2 to 3 weeks or more | Most openings, lots of highway miles to build skill | Long stretches away from home |
| OTR refrigerated (reefer) | Very often | Out 2 to 3 weeks or more | Steady freight year-round | Tight appointment times, more waiting |
| Regional dry van or reefer | Often | Home weekly or most weekends | Better home time than OTR | Fewer openings for zero experience |
| Dedicated accounts | Sometimes | Often weekly or better | Consistent routes and customers | Some involve touch freight or many stops |
| Team driving | Often | Varies | Can pay well and build miles fast | Sharing a truck is not for everyone |
| LTL and local P and D | Occasionally | Home daily | Home every night | Usually prefer experience; city driving is hard |
| Tanker, hazmat, and flatbed | Occasionally | Varies | Higher pay potential | Harder skill set; more risk for a new driver |

To understand home time in each lane, see our comparison of [OTR vs regional vs local trucking jobs](/blog/otr-vs-regional-vs-local-trucking-jobs).

### OTR dry van and reefer: the most common starting point

Long-haul dry van and reefer jobs hire the largest share of new CDL drivers. The driving is mostly highway, which is the safest place to build skill with a large vehicle. Reefer freight tends to be steady, but it comes with strict delivery windows and more waiting at food distribution centers.

### Regional and dedicated: better home time if you can get it

Regional and dedicated jobs that accept new drivers are worth pursuing if home time matters to you. Ask carefully about the freight. Some dedicated accounts involve backing into tight retail docks or unloading freight by hand.

### Endorsements can open more doors

Adding tanker, doubles and triples, or hazmat endorsements can make you eligible for more jobs, although some carriers still require experience before assigning that freight. Each endorsement requires a knowledge test, and hazmat also requires a TSA background check.

## How company-sponsored CDL training works

If you do not have your CDL yet, company-sponsored training is one of the most common paths into trucking. Our [step-by-step CDL guide](/blog/how-to-get-your-cdl-step-by-step-guide) covers the licensing process, including federal Entry-Level Driver Training (ELDT) requirements. Under FMCSA rules, most new Class A and Class B applicants must complete training from a provider listed on the [FMCSA Training Provider Registry](https://tpr.fmcsa.dot.gov/) before taking certain CDL skills tests.

### The common sponsorship models

| Model | How it works | What to watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carrier-run school | Carrier trains you in-house, then you drive for them | Contract length and repayment if you leave |
| Carrier-paid outside school | Carrier pays a partner school, then hires you | Whether you owe the full tuition if you do not finish or leave |
| Tuition reimbursement | You pay for school, carrier reimburses you over time | How much per month, and whether it stops if you leave |
| Private school, then apply | You pay yourself and choose any carrier | Higher up-front cost, but no commitment |

### Questions to ask about any training agreement

1. What is the **total dollar amount** I could owe?
2. How long must I work for the company, and does the balance **decrease each month**?
3. What happens if I am **terminated** rather than quitting?
4. Am I **paid during training**, and how much?
5. How long do I ride with a trainer, and can I request a different trainer if there is a problem?
6. After training, am I placed as a **solo driver** or on a team?

Get every answer in writing. For a full list of questions to use on the phone, see our guide on [questions to ask a trucking recruiter](/blog/questions-to-ask-a-trucking-recruiter).

## What to look for in your first trucking job

Starting pay for new drivers is usually lower than for experienced drivers, so focus on the factors that protect your paycheck, your safety, and your future.

### Must-haves

- **Paid training** with a clear timeline and pay rate
- **Written pay terms**, including the mileage method and detention pay
- **Reasonable weekly miles**, based on what current new drivers actually run
- **Benefits** that start within a reasonable time
- A **realistic home time** policy that is stated clearly

### Nice-to-haves

- Automatic transmissions, which many carriers now use
- APUs or inverters for comfort during breaks
- A dedicated driver manager with a manageable number of drivers
- A clear path to regional or dedicated work after a set period

### Warning signs for new drivers

- Being pushed toward a **lease-purchase** program right out of school. Our [owner operator vs company driver guide](/blog/owner-operator-vs-company-driver) explains the risks.
- Training contracts with unclear or very high repayment terms
- Unpaid orientation or unpaid time with a trainer
- Promises of top-tier pay for first-year drivers

## How much do new truck drivers make?

First-year pay depends on the carrier, freight type, region, and how many miles you get. New drivers commonly start below the industry median and see raises as they build experience and a clean safety record. For broader wage data, check the [Bureau of Labor Statistics page for heavy and tractor-trailer truck drivers](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/transportation-and-material-moving/heavy-and-tractor-trailer-truck-drivers.htm).

**A simple way to estimate first-year pay:** multiply the starting rate by realistic weekly miles and working weeks.

- Example: $0.50 per mile times 2,200 miles times 48 weeks equals **$52,800 per year**
- If weekly miles average 1,800 instead: $0.50 times 1,800 times 48 equals **$43,200 per year**

Those are illustrative numbers, but they show why weekly miles matter as much as the rate.

## First-year survival tips for new CDL drivers

The first year is the hardest. Drivers who make it through usually have better options, higher pay, and more choice over home time.

### Protect your safety record

- **Slow down.** Most early accidents are low-speed backing incidents and tight turns. Get out and look as many times as you need to.
- **Plan parking early.** Truck parking fills up fast in the evening. Plan your stop before your hours run low.
- **Know your hours.** Understand the 11-hour driving limit, the 14-hour window, and the 30-minute break. FMCSA publishes the full rules if you want the details.
- **Never drive fatigued** to make an appointment. Tell dispatch early if a load cannot be delivered safely on time.

### Manage your money

- Build a small **emergency fund** as quickly as you can.
- Pack food and use a small cooler or fridge. Eating every meal at truck stops adds up quickly.
- Check each **settlement** against your trip records and ask about anything that looks wrong.
- Learn how **per diem** works at your company and how it affects your taxes.

### Take care of your health

- Keep a regular sleep routine as much as your schedule allows.
- Walk during breaks and fuel stops, even for 10 minutes.
- Stay on top of your DOT medical card renewal date.

### Build a good relationship with dispatch

- Communicate early and clearly about delays, breakdowns, and hours.
- Be reliable, and ask for what you need, such as home time or more miles, in a direct and polite way.
- Keep notes of promises and agreements in case you need to follow up.

### Plan your next move

- Aim for about **one year of clean, verifiable experience**. Many better-paying regional, local, and specialized jobs open up after that.
- Keep copies of your employment dates and training records.
- Think about which endorsements or freight types interest you for year two.
